Until relatively recently, my understanding of great classical literature was it served no real practical purpose. Its limited purpose in life was to serve as cultural time capsules---and people that studied literature in school were equivalent to cultural archaeologists (for the record, archaeologists are pretty cool). I had heard of some of the great authors---like Charles Dickens, Jane Austen, Leo Tolstoy, and titles like <em>War and Peace</em>, <em>Moby Dick</em>, and <em>The Count of Monte Cristo</em>---but my interest had never gone deeper than name recognition, and I dodged reading any of them in school as a kid. I have been a relatively active non-fiction reader most of my adult life, and I assumed anything of pratical value was held in that domain. So what changed?

One of the beautiful curses of being committed to taking curiosity seriously is that it can open very unexpected doors in life. This past summer (of 2025), I took a humanities class to fill a needed elective for the Psychology undergraduate degree I've been working towards. This humanities class had us read some excerpts from some of the great pieces of literature throughout history. But more importantly, we were reading these excerpts with the lens of <em>great art having the capability to illuminate human truths that science and reason can't</em> quite get us to. This was the light bulb moment for me.

As a person that has always been driven by curiosity, generally in an effort to cultivate contentment, I've generally thought of art as a type of emotional sideshow. That is to say, it can have some cathartic utility, but it doesn't generally help us pursue truth and virtue. The lightbulb moment in this humanities 101 class was connecting the dots between exploring immaterial truths---like love, beauty, justice, etc.---and creative works, like great literature. These dots connecting was one of those moments where an electrical circuit completed and my curiosity ran right up to the dusty door labeled, "Classical Literature" and blew it wide open.

## You Have My Attention

The way I often approach a new curiosity-driven project in life is by first consuming as much relevant information as I can about the subject---and it's no different with reading literature. My intention with reading great literature is to use it as a mirror for peering into my own life and mind through the lens of the greatest writers in history. 

The gift that literature (and other artistic mediums) offers is that it can indirectly illuminate human truths that reason alone can't capture. When I'm peering into the mirror of literature, implicit in my intenion is questions like, <mark><em>How is this helping me understand </em>justice<em> with more clarity?</em></mark> The same can be asked for concepts like <em>love</em>, <em>beauty</em>, <em>kindness</em>, and any other concept that only wisdom can address. 

Good literature offers us the opportunity to sit with life's most important questions---it offers us the opportunity to <em>live</em> life's most important questions in more dimensions than the single one we wake up in every morning. In this way, great literature is the opposite of escapism. Great literature---when intentionally and mindfully engaged with---helps us show up in our daily lives with greater capacities for empathy, kindness, justice, patience, forgiveness, and all the other virtues we typically attribute to a good person.
